Tery is our Jack Russell. He is five months old, weighs 6.3 kg, and he is changing his teeth, so everything in reach gets chewed: toys, slippers, plastic bottles, pinecones from the woods. On 18 November 2026 we gathered every toy he owns, eight in total, put them on the floor and looked at each one honestly. Three still look almost new after two months of daily use. Three need watching. Two went in the bin.

This article is that inventory, with a photo of every toy in the state it is in today. Nothing here is a lab test or a sponsored pick, just what one small, very determined terrier did to his toys.

What a Jack Russell puppy actually does with a toy

Watching Tery play says more about which toys work than any product description. He does three things with a toy:

  • Carries it. He picks a toy up and runs across the terrace with it, or carries it around the house.
  • Shakes and attacks it. Plastic bottles are a favourite target, and so are slippers he steals. Grab, shake, pin down: the way terriers were bred to go after prey.
  • Chews it. This is where toys die. A puppy his age is changing his teeth and chews to soothe his gums, and chewing is what wore down every damaged toy in this article.

A good toy for a Jack Russell has to survive all three. In our house it is the chewing that sorted the toys into "still fine" and "bin".

What survived: three toys after two months

These three toys have been in daily use for about two months and show no visible damage. All three are made of solid, firm rubber, which is the one thing they have in common.

What we are watching: ropes and a chewed bone

These three are still usable, but each one shows the kind of wear that can turn into a problem, so we keep checking them.

Rope with a cream rubber bone, and a blue rubber bone

The rope is strong, but the tassels at one end have become long and loose. We trim them with scissors so there are no long strands he can pull off and swallow. The cream rubber bone on the rope is still whole.

The separate blue rubber bone is different: both ends are chewed down. It is still in one piece, but it goes in the bin the moment small pieces start coming off.

Orange and blue knotted rope

The body and the knots are still tight, but the ends are frayed and spreading out. Same treatment: trim the tassels and keep an eye on it.

What went in the bin, and why

Two toys did not survive. Both failed in a way that is worth understanding, because the risk is not that the toy is ruined but what the puppy might swallow.

Red and white football

{{img:03}}

The outer layer is chewed through and the foam underneath is exposed. Foam comes off in small pieces that are easy to swallow, and swallowed pieces can block the intestines. It looked like a sturdy ball when it was new. Once the outer layer was broken, it stopped being a toy.

Pastel rope with knot ends

{{img:07}}

Badly stretched, with one end dark and coming apart. Swallowed rope fibres are a known danger for dogs: a long thread can act as what vets call a linear foreign body and damage the intestines as it moves through. A rope in this state is not worth keeping.

Rope toys: only for tug together

The one rule we took away from this inventory: ropes are for playing tug together, not for leaving with the dog unsupervised. Tug is what ropes are good for. Chewing alone is what frays them, and every frayed strand is something he could swallow. After a game, the rope goes away.

Rubber toys are the opposite. The three that survived are the ones he is allowed to have on his own.

How to check your Jack Russell's toys

The check we now do takes less than a minute:

  • Rubber toys: look for cracks, missing chunks, or ends chewed down. If small pieces are coming off, the toy goes.
  • Toys with a coating or a soft layer: once the outer layer is broken and you can see foam or filling, bin it. Our football is the example.
  • Ropes: trim long, loose tassels. If the rope is coming apart or getting dark and stringy, bin it.
  • Size: at 6.3 kg Tery handles all of these comfortably. The toy should be easy for him to carry but impossible to swallow whole, and he will keep growing, so we keep checking.

If your terrier is younger or a heavy chewer, it is worth starting with the solid rubber options. More on the breed's need to chase and grab in our guide to the Jack Russell prey drive, and on everyday training in the Jack Russell training guide.

Conclusion

After two months with a teething Jack Russell puppy, the pattern in our house is simple. Solid rubber toys survived: the spiky ball, the treat ball and the ring. Ropes work, but only for tug together and with trimmed tassels. Anything with a soft layer under a hard shell, like our football, lasts only until he breaks through it, and then it becomes a hazard. We will update this page as Tery gets older and his adult teeth come in.

FAQ

What toys are safe for a Jack Russell puppy to chew alone?

In our experience, solid, firm rubber toys: a spiky ball, a treat-dispensing ball and a rubber ring all lasted two months of daily chewing by our 5-month-old Jack Russell without visible damage. Check them regularly anyway and remove any toy that starts losing pieces.

Are rope toys safe for Jack Russell Terriers?

Ropes are fine for supervised tug games, but they should not be left with the dog to chew alone. Loose fibres can be swallowed, and a long thread can cause serious intestinal damage. Trim long tassels and throw the rope away once it starts coming apart.

When should I throw a dog toy away?

As soon as pieces start coming off, when a coated toy is chewed through to the foam or filling underneath, or when a rope is badly frayed and stringy. A damaged toy is a swallowing risk, not just a worn-out toy.

Why does my Jack Russell puppy chew everything?

Puppies change their teeth at around four to six months, and chewing soothes their gums. Jack Russells also have a strong drive to grab, shake and carry things. Giving them durable toys they are allowed to chew helps keep slippers and bottles safe.
